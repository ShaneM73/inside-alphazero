Each move travels through the whole network. Watch the sheets shift back in time, the filters light up, the signal ripple through the 19 blocks, and the two heads answer.

Tap a piece on this board (or the 3D one), then a highlighted square, to play your own move from any position. The sample game is Morphy's Opera Game (Paris, 1858), which ends in checkmate on move 17.

Layer shapes match AlphaZero's 2018 paper. Its trained weights were never released, so the network here runs with random weights: it shows how signals flow, not what AlphaZero learned.

Each 14-sheet group is one moment in time; groups further right are older. When a move is played, every group slides one slot back and a fresh "now" group pops in. Tap a lit cell in the 3D stack to jump to its sheet. AlphaZero flips the board so the side to move is always at the bottom; this demo keeps White at the bottom so the sheets are easier to follow.

A filter you can read

A 3×3 window slides over every square, multiplies its weights by the sheets underneath, adds a bias, and keeps the result only if it's above zero.

The pawn weights are 0.5 so the filter only fires when a pawn and a target are both present. Tap a square on the board to test it.

Features that spread

Each block has two 3×3 convolutions and adds its input back (the arcs above the blocks). The board overlay shows how far one square can "see" at this depth; tap the board to move it. The tower runs 32 random channels instead of AlphaZero's 256 trained ones.

4,672 move slots

A move is named by its starting square plus one of 73 sheets that says which way and how far it goes. The lit cells in the 3D stack are the slots that are legal right now; every other slot is masked out.

Sheets 0–55 are slides (8 directions × 1 to 7 squares), 56–63 knight jumps, 64–72 promotions to knight, bishop or rook. The exact sheet order is illustrative. Tap the board to choose a starting square.

How good is this position?

The value head shrinks the tower's output to one sheet, passes it through a 256-unit layer, and squashes the result into one number between −1 and +1, from the point of view of the side to move.

−1 loss0 draw+1 win

With random weights the real head would print noise, so the gauge here is driven by a stand-in: material count squashed with tanh, and ±1 at checkmate. A trained AlphaZero judges far more than material.
